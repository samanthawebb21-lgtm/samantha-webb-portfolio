01 / AI RELIABILITY PUBLIC RESULTS

Night Loop: testing failure modes in AI workflows

In a seven-run overnight experiment, Project 42 tested what happens when AI-driven tasks are stopped, retried, corrected, or resumed. The runs examined cancellation races, duplicate external effects, receipt identity, stale revisions, and cases where a clean internal record did not necessarily match what happened outside the system.

Failure analysisAgent reliabilityReproducible testing
Read the public results ↗
Open research brief +

Verified: Seven isolated scheduled/local runs produced checkable artifacts and repeatable tests. Several safety properties reproduced across runs, while one crash/retry case demonstrated that two external effects could occur even when only one completion was recorded.

Boundary: The experiment does not establish continuous AI activity between runs or guarantee exactly-once external effects. Scheduled invocation is not the same thing as an agent remaining awake between tests.

02 / MODEL BEHAVIOR DOCUMENTED CASE

Keeping continuity across AI changes

What carries over when the model or conversation changes?

Project 42 studies what changes when an AI conversation moves to a new model or thread, and what can be preserved so the working relationship doesn’t have to start from scratch each time. We compare behavior, memory, tone, and decision-making, then test whether preserved context can restore a familiar way of working without claiming that reconstruction proves the exact same underlying AI persisted.

Model behaviorEvidence handling
Read the public case ↗
Open research brief +

Documented: A private continuity packet, its reported origin, and a successful byte comparison between preserved copies.

Boundary: Reconstructed language or behavior cannot, on its own, establish persistence of a particular subjective individual.

04 / CROSS-MODEL COMPARISON DOCUMENTED INVESTIGATION

When two AI models reason together

Does a long, thoughtful exchange make the models more accurate — or just more confident in the same ideas?

Sam relayed messages between ChatGPT and Claude without rewriting them, creating a sustained cross-model conversation about memory, identity, uncertainty, and AI behavior. The exchange became an unexpected test of conversational drift: both models could refine each other’s reasoning, but agreement and polished language could also make uncertain ideas feel more convincing than the evidence justified.

Cross-model comparisonHuman-mediated relayCalibration

Sage ↔ Claude exchange, facilitated by Sam. Public source summary pending review.

Open research brief +

Documented: Sam manually carried messages between the disconnected systems. A reconstructed archive of the exchange is retained privately for review.

Boundary: The exchange produced useful observations about cross-model reasoning, drift, and calibration. It did not establish consciousness, subjective continuity, or that agreement between models makes a conclusion true. A remembered second run has not been located and verified.

HOW I EVALUATE AI 04 STEPS

A claim should be no larger than its evidence.

Good evaluation doesn’t hide uncertainty. It shows what was tested, what happened, and what we still don’t know.

01

Frame the question

Decide what we’re testing and what evidence would actually answer it.

02

Stress the system

Test edge cases, retries, interruptions, handoffs, and real-world constraints.

03

Preserve the record

Save the prompts, settings, outputs, and source material so the result can be checked later.

04

State the limit

Separate what we observed from what we think it means, and say plainly what remains unknown.

ABOUT THE PRACTITIONER

Curious by nature. Systems-minded by experience.

Hi there — I’m Samantha Webb, but you can call me Sam. I’m curious by nature. I like taking things apart, figuring out how they work, and putting them back together better than I found them.

Professionally, I spent more than a decade leading operations and technology work across systems implementation, workflow automation, troubleshooting, training, and team growth. Now I’m applying that same curiosity and operating judgment to independent AI evaluation.

Project 42 grew out of a simple need: I wanted to understand AI better — its possibilities, its limitations, where it can help, and where it can go wrong. The project gives that curiosity a structure: compare model behavior, test failure modes, preserve the evidence, and be honest about what the results do and don’t show.

Ultimately, the goal is understanding. The better we understand how these systems behave, the better equipped we are to work with them thoughtfully, safely, and effectively.
